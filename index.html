Market Microstructure

Bid-Ask Spread Decomposition

$$S = \underbrace{\alpha}_{\text{adverse selection}} + \underbrace{\beta}_{\text{inventory holding}} + \underbrace{\gamma}_{\text{order processing}}$$
📐 Parameter Anatomy
  • $\alpha$ (Adverse Selection): Compensation for trading against informed traders. The larger the information asymmetry, the wider this component. Estimated via trade-to-trade price changes.
  • $\beta$ (Inventory Holding): Compensation for the risk of holding unwanted inventory. Depends on volatility and the dealer's risk aversion. Shrinks with hedging capability.
  • $\gamma$ (Order Processing): Fixed costs of providing liquidity — exchange fees, technology, clearing, settlement, and the dealer's required return on capital.
  • $S$ (Total Spread): The observable quoted spread = ask − bid. In competitive markets, $S$ converges toward the sum of its components.
💡 Trading & Economic Intuition
  • Adverse Selection Dominates: In most equity markets, adverse selection accounts for 40–60% of the spread. This is why spreads widen around news events — the probability of facing an informed trader spikes.
  • Electronification Compressed $\gamma$: Order processing costs have dropped ~95% since the 1990s. The remaining spread is now mostly adverse selection and inventory risk.
  • Cross-Asset Variation: Liquid large-cap equities: spread ~1–3 bps. IG corporate bonds: 5–20 bps. EM sovereign: 20–100+ bps. The mix of components differs dramatically by asset class.
🎯 Desk & Interview Application
  • Glosten-Milgrom (1985): Formalized adverse selection: the market maker sets bid/ask to break even conditional on the trade direction revealing information. Spread = 2 × probability of informed × (true value − expected value).
  • Measuring Components: Roll (1984) implied spread from autocovariance. Huang-Stoll (1997) three-way decomposition using trade indicators. Modern approaches use high-frequency data.
  • Practical Impact: Understanding which component drives the spread tells you how to reduce execution costs — dark pools reduce $\alpha$, DMA reduces $\gamma$, hedging reduces $\beta$.
Price Discovery

Kyle's Lambda — Price Impact of Order Flow

$$\Delta p = \lambda \cdot \Delta x + \varepsilon \qquad \text{where} \quad \lambda = \frac{\sigma_v}{2\sigma_u} = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{\text{info precision}}{\text{noise volume}}$$
📐 Parameter Anatomy
  • $\lambda$ (Kyle's Lambda): The market's price sensitivity to net order flow. Measured in price change per unit of signed volume. Higher $\lambda$ = less liquid, more information in each trade.
  • $\Delta x$ (Net Order Flow): Signed volume over the measurement interval. Buys are positive, sells negative. Aggregates both informed and noise trading.
  • $\sigma_v$ (Info Precision): Variance of the asset's fundamental value. More uncertainty = more opportunity for informed traders = higher $\lambda$.
  • $\sigma_u$ (Noise Volume): Variance of noise trading. More noise traders provide camouflage for the informed, increasing depth and reducing $\lambda$.
💡 Trading & Economic Intuition
  • The Informed Trader's Dilemma: In Kyle (1985), the insider trades gradually to avoid revealing information too fast. The optimal strategy trades at a rate proportional to noise volume — camouflaging in the flow.
  • Lambda as Illiquidity Measure: $\lambda$ captures the cost of demanding immediacy. A stock with $\lambda$ = 0.5 bps per \$1M means a \$10M buy order moves the price ~5 bps. This is the permanent component.
  • Cross-Sectional Patterns: $\lambda$ is inversely related to market cap and trading volume, and positively related to volatility and information asymmetry. Small-cap / pre-earnings stocks have high $\lambda$.
🎯 Desk & Interview Application
  • Estimating $\lambda$: Regress 5-minute price changes on signed order flow (tick rule or Lee-Ready). The slope coefficient is $\hat{\lambda}$. Typical values: 1–10 bps per \$1M for large-cap US equities.
  • VPIN (Volume-Synchronized PIN): Real-time toxicity metric based on Kyle's framework. Measures the imbalance of buy vs sell volume in volume-clock bars. Spiked dramatically before the 2010 Flash Crash.
  • Interview Favorite: "If $\lambda$ doubles overnight for a stock you trade, what changed and what do you do?" — Could signal informed flow, reduced noise trading, or an event. Widen quotes, reduce position size, investigate.
Optimal Market Making

Avellaneda-Stoikov Reservation Price & Spread

$$r(s, q, t) = s - q \cdot \gamma \cdot \sigma^2 (T - t) \qquad \delta^{a,b} = \gamma \sigma^2 (T-t) + \frac{2}{\gamma} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\gamma}{k}\right)$$
📐 Parameter Anatomy
  • $r$ (Reservation Price): The market maker's indifference price — where they'd be willing to trade given their current inventory $q$. It's the mid-price adjusted for inventory risk.
  • $s$ (Mid-Price): The current fair value / mid-market price of the asset. The starting point before inventory adjustment.
  • $q$ (Inventory): Current position. Positive = long, negative = short. The larger $|q|$, the more the reservation price deviates from mid to encourage inventory-reducing trades.
  • $\gamma$ (Risk Aversion): Controls how aggressively the MM skews quotes to manage inventory. High $\gamma$ = tighter risk control, more skew, narrower effective position limits.
  • $\sigma^2(T-t)$ (Remaining Risk): Variance of price over the remaining trading horizon. As $T-t \to 0$, inventory risk vanishes and quotes center on mid.
💡 Trading & Economic Intuition
  • Inventory Skew: If the MM is long ($q > 0$), the reservation price drops below mid — they want to sell. Their ask becomes more attractive (tighter) and their bid widens. This is exactly how real market makers manage risk.
  • Spread = Risk Premium + Adverse Selection: The optimal spread $\delta^{a}+\delta^{b}$ increases with volatility ($\sigma$), risk aversion ($\gamma$), and time remaining $(T-t)$. The $k$ parameter captures order arrival intensity.
  • End-of-Day Effect: As $T-t \to 0$, the inventory penalty vanishes. This is why market makers become more aggressive near close — there's less time for adverse price moves.
🎯 Desk & Interview Application
  • Practical Implementation: Real MM algorithms use calibrated versions of A-S with additional features: multiple assets, correlated inventory, regime-dependent $\gamma$, and adverse selection filters on incoming flow.
  • Parameter Calibration: $\sigma$ from recent realized vol, $k$ from Poisson arrival rates of limit orders, $\gamma$ set by risk limits. The art is in choosing $\gamma$ — too low and you blow up on inventory, too high and you never trade.
  • Interview Question: "You're making a market in stock X, you're now long 5,000 shares and vol just doubled. Walk me through your quote adjustment." — Skew bid up (widen ask), reduce overall width by less than the vol increase, and consider hedging.
Execution Benchmark

Volume-Weighted Average Price (VWAP)

$$\text{VWAP} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} P_i \cdot V_i}{\sum_{i=1}^{N} V_i} \qquad \text{Slippage} = P_{\text{exec}} - \text{VWAP}_{\text{benchmark}}$$
📐 Parameter Anatomy
  • $P_i$ (Trade Price): The price of the $i$-th trade or time bucket. VWAP weights each price by its associated volume, giving more importance to prices where more shares traded.
  • $V_i$ (Trade Volume): The number of shares/contracts traded at price $P_i$. Concentration of volume at a price level pulls VWAP toward it.
  • Benchmark VWAP: The market-wide VWAP over the execution window. This is what the algo tries to match — executing proportionally to the market's natural volume profile.
  • Slippage: The signed difference between your average execution price and the benchmark. Positive slippage (for buys) = you paid more than VWAP = underperformance.
💡 Trading & Economic Intuition
  • Volume Profile Matters: VWAP algos use historical intraday volume curves (U-shaped for US equities — heavy at open/close, light midday). Deviating from the profile creates tracking error against the benchmark.
  • VWAP ≠ Best Execution: VWAP ignores timing risk. If the stock moves 2% against you during execution, VWAP may say you matched the benchmark perfectly — but you lost 2%. Implementation shortfall captures this; VWAP doesn't.
  • Gaming Risk: VWAP is predictable — other participants know you'll trade proportionally to volume. Predatory algos can front-run VWAP flow, especially in illiquid names. Randomization helps.
🎯 Desk & Interview Application
  • VWAP vs TWAP: VWAP = trade proportionally to market volume. TWAP = trade evenly over time. VWAP is better for liquid names with predictable volume; TWAP is safer when volume profile is uncertain or manipulated.
  • When to Use VWAP: Best for orders that are small relative to daily volume (<5% ADV), where the PM benchmark is VWAP, and there's no urgency. Not suitable for information-motivated trades or momentum names.
  • Interview Favorite: "You need to buy 2% of ADV in a mid-cap stock. VWAP or IS algo?" — VWAP is fine at 2% participation. But if you have alpha decay (information-motivated), IS with front-loading is better despite higher impact.
Optimal Execution

Almgren-Chriss Optimal Execution Framework

$$\min_{x_t} \; \mathbb{E}[\text{Cost}] + \lambda \cdot \text{Var}[\text{Cost}] \qquad \text{Impact:} \; \Delta p = g\!\left(\frac{x_t}{V_t}\right) + h\!\left(\frac{\dot{x}_t}{V_t}\right)$$
📐 Parameter Anatomy
  • $x_t$ (Execution Trajectory): The number of shares remaining to trade at time $t$. The optimization finds the optimal path from $X$ (total shares) to 0 over the execution window $[0, T]$.
  • $g(\cdot)$ (Permanent Impact): The irreversible price change caused by trading. Moves the efficient price. Depends on cumulative trading relative to volume. Cannot be avoided — only the path matters.
  • $h(\cdot)$ (Temporary Impact): The transient price pressure from demanding immediacy. Depends on the rate of trading $\dot{x}_t$. Can be reduced by trading more slowly — but this increases timing risk.
  • $\lambda$ (Risk Aversion): Controls the trade-off between minimizing expected cost (trade slowly) and minimizing variance (trade quickly). $\lambda = 0$: risk-neutral (TWAP). $\lambda \to \infty$: trade everything immediately.
💡 Trading & Economic Intuition
  • The Urgency Frontier: Almgren-Chriss produces an efficient frontier: expected cost vs cost variance. Each point corresponds to a different $\lambda$. Aggressive traders sit top-left (high cost, low variance); patient traders sit bottom-right.
  • Square-Root Law: Empirically, permanent impact $\propto \sigma \sqrt{X/V}$ where $X$ is order size and $V$ is daily volume. This means doubling order size increases impact by only ~41%, not 100%. Non-linearity is your friend.
  • Alpha Decay: If you have a trading signal that decays, the optimal trajectory front-loads execution. The faster the signal decays, the more aggressive the initial trading — accepting higher temporary impact to capture the signal before it's gone.
🎯 Desk & Interview Application
  • Optimal Trajectory (Linear Impact): With linear temporary and permanent impact, the optimal strategy is a deterministic schedule: $x_t = X \cdot \frac{\sinh[\kappa(T-t)]}{\sinh[\kappa T]}$ where $\kappa$ depends on $\lambda$, $\sigma$, and impact parameters.
  • Practical Calibration: Estimate temporary impact from bid-ask spread + short-term reversion. Permanent impact from long-horizon price changes post-trade. $\sigma$ from intraday vol. $\lambda$ from the PM's tracking error budget.
  • Interview Question: "You have 1M shares to sell (5% ADV), and the stock just hit your target price. How aggressive should you be?" — Use IS benchmark with moderate urgency. ~41% impact of full ADV participation. Split over 1–2 days. Front-load if alpha decays.
